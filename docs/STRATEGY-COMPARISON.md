# Coulis: earlier product vs. current research-lab vision

Decision memo · October 31, 2026

## Recommendation

Keep the original visual identity and the current research-lab ambition. Use the writing prototype as the first instrument for testing and selling the research thesis. The earlier product is easier to validate and monetize in the near term; the current vision offers a broader potential business, but has substantially more technical, distribution, and financing risk. A larger narrative is not itself evidence of a stronger business.

“Earlier” means the personal writing/taste checker, including its already-stated ambition to become infrastructure. “Current” means a frontier research lab developing a taste LLM, distributed through B2B2C integrations, with paid partnerships and venture funding from the outset. This is a change in emphasis and business architecture, not a completely unrelated idea.

## SWOT: earlier personal taste product

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Strengths | Concrete workflow, existing prototype, a short path to observing user behavior, and direct access to sentence-level edits and approvals. Easier to demonstrate what changes for a user today. |
| Weaknesses | No repeat-use or time-saving evidence yet. A checker can feel like extra work. Broad support for many writing formats can weaken focus. Rules and prompts alone are relatively easy to reproduce. |
| Opportunities | Own a frequent writing workflow, establish an agency/team distribution channel, and learn which preferences persist across topics. A narrow utility can become a useful research interface. |
| Threats | Bundling by general assistants, low willingness to pay for a second writing tool, inconsistent provider quality, and users abandoning setup before seeing value. |

## SWOT: current B2B2C taste-model research lab

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Strengths | A coherent long-term question: can models learn individual judgment rather than generic quality? Partner integrations could distribute the capability across existing products. Paid work can ground research in real workflows. These are structural advantages if executed, not proven outcomes. |
| Weaknesses | No trained model, published preference win, or independent adoption evidence yet. More demanding technical hiring and research execution. Slow integrations may obscure user feedback. Model training and data rights add operational complexity. |
| Opportunities | Build evaluation and personalization capabilities that product teams prefer to buy. Develop context-aware generation, ranking, or critique on licensed base models. Create a permissioned longitudinal dataset that demonstrates transfer to unseen topics. |
| Threats | Frontier-model improvements, competitors with established APIs, research results that fail to generalize, customer reluctance to share data, concentrated partner revenue, and dependence on future fundraising. |

## Viability assessment

These are qualitative judgments, not probabilities or investment advice.

**Near-term commercial viability: earlier product has the advantage.** Its value can be tested with a smaller implementation and shorter feedback cycle. That does not establish willingness to pay; no tests have been reported. The current route can also collect early revenue, but only if a buyer values the defined partnership deliverable enough to pay for it.

**Long-term infrastructure potential: current vision has the advantage if the research works.** Embedded distribution and reusable model capabilities can create a larger business than a stand-alone checker. The challenge is proving that a customer should buy Coulis instead of adding preferences, examples, and memory to its existing model stack.

**Research viability: plausible hypothesis, unresolved outcome.** Start by comparing an existing model with a strong context/retrieval baseline against a personalized ranker, adapter, or post-trained model. Use unseen topics and context changes, not just remembered examples. Do not assume training a foundation model from scratch is the best route to a taste LLM.

**Day-one revenue: commercial policy, not a forecast.** Charge for scoped pilots when you have a buyer and deliverable. Set boundaries on customization so revenue does not turn the lab into an agency. Track engineering hours, provider costs, repeatability, conversion to recurring usage, and partner retention.

**VC-first: compatible with early revenue.** Raise against a research thesis, technical ability, distribution access, and milestones. Revenue helps when it demonstrates recurring demand; bespoke service revenue alone does not validate infrastructure economics. A research program whose next experiment depends entirely on closing a round has a financing bottleneck.

## YC viability

Neither idea can be assigned a defensible acceptance percentage from the information available. YC explicitly accepts idea-stage companies and says founders should have the skills to build the product themselves. Lack of revenue is not an automatic disqualifier; missing evidence about the founding team's technical capability remains material. [YC FAQ](https://www.ycombinator.com/faq)

The earlier idea is easier to demonstrate but risks looking like a replaceable feature. The current idea is more ambitious but risks looking like a relabeled wrapper unless the application explains the original research, technical plan, and evidence. Calling the company a frontier lab raises expectations; it does not meet them.

The strongest current application would join the two: “We are building taste infrastructure for AI, starting with personalized writing judgment. Our local prototype lets us test explicit preferences and context; our research will test whether learned judgment improves unseen outputs beyond strong prompted baselines.” State actual usage, revenue, and model status separately; do not fill missing numbers with forecasts.

## Competitive implication

Taste Labs already describes itself as a taste-infrastructure company, with a design starting point and a Brand API. Its website also describes both model-training and app/agent infrastructure work. These are its own claims, not independently verified performance. This makes “taste infrastructure” a contested category, not an exclusive positioning advantage. Coulis needs a specific demonstrated difference: for example, individual writing preferences that transfer across topics and respect changing context. [Taste Labs](https://tastelabs.com/)

## What would change the assessment?

1. **A real buyer:** one paid AI-product partnership with an agreed workflow, decision-maker, measurable success criterion, and rollout path. Target, not achieved traction.
2. **Repeated use:** partner users return without founder assistance. Measure active users and cohorts, not only trial signups or drafts generated.
3. **Controlled benefit:** blind preference comparisons and editing-time measurements against a strong general-model baseline, with the same input context and comparable latency/cost.
4. **Transfer:** hold out topics and test audience/context shifts. Separate retrieval improvements from model-weight improvements.
5. **Data rights:** permission for operating the customer's product must not silently become permission for pooled training. Keep approvals, withdrawal handling, and isolation explicit.
6. **Repeatable economics:** a second integration reuses the first integration's core work and yields recurring usage rather than another bespoke project.

Suggested first experiment: one partner, one recurring writing workflow, consented users, baseline and personalized variants, and a prespecified evaluation. Sample size should follow the effect size and reliability required; a small pilot is discovery, not a universal quality benchmark.

The recommended company remains the research lab. The earlier product remains its first testable application and route to customer evidence.
